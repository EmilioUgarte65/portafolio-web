AI Research & Benchmarking

Breaking the VRAM wall: powerful models with less compute.

An applied research lab for running LLMs (large language models, the AI that generates text) on a laptop GPU with 4 GB of VRAM, the graphics card's memory. The core tool is quantization: storing each model weight with fewer bits so it takes less space, while measuring how much quality is lost at every step.

The Core Objective

A 7B model (7 billion parameters) in FP16 weighs ~14 GB: it doesn't fit in 4 GB, spills into system RAM over the PCIe bus, and speed collapses. At ~2.7 bits per weight it would take ~2.4 GB and fit entirely on the GPU. That is the target; this is what the lab has already measured:

In plain words

What it is and why it matters

What is quantization?

A model is a huge list of numbers called weights. Each one normally takes 16 bits (FP16). Quantizing means rounding them to fewer levels: 4 bits allow 16 possible values (INT4) and 2 bits only 4 (INT2). It's like reducing the colors of a photo: much smaller, but go too far and it shows.

The problem: the 4 GB wall

Somewhere between 3B and 7B parameters there is a wall: the model no longer fits in VRAM and every token has to cross the PCIe bus from system RAM. On this laptop a 3B model generates 82.6 tokens per second; a 27B model that doesn't fit, 1.1.

How it attacks it

Using a small lab model (GPT-2, 124 million parameters), each technique is tested on its own, its quality is measured, and the ones that add up are stacked. The goal: 4-bit quality at a 2-bit size.

Step by step

How the research works

Every idea goes through the same testbed, so the numbers can be compared.

Measure the baseline

A benchmark calls the Ollama API (a program that runs LLMs on your own machine) and logs tokens per second, load time, and whether the model runs on the GPU or the CPU.

Quantize

Each technique lives in its own PyTorch script with CUDA (NVIDIA's GPU computing platform): uniform, Lloyd-Max, GPTQ, rotations and patches.

Measure quality

Quality is measured with perplexity (PPL), how "surprised" the model is by a text: lower is better. It's reported as a ratio against the original (1.00× = no loss), with separate calibration and evaluation texts.

Stack and validate

Techniques that add up are combined. Key results are re-run (the 12.45× was validated twice) and tried on larger models.

Methodology & Findings

What the lab notebook uncovered

Finding 1 · Naive quantization

Careless rounding wrecks the model

The first 4-bit attempt made perplexity 32,870 times worse. There were two bugs: GPT-2 stores its weights transposed (Conv1D layers), so they were quantized along the wrong axis, and sensitive layers were touched (the embeddings, which turn words into numbers, and the output layer). With both fixes together, INT4 dropped to 1.14×.

Finding 2 · Lloyd-Max

Put the levels where the weights are

Lloyd-Max is a non-uniform quantizer (1-D k-means): it places the levels where real weights cluster instead of at equal spacing. INT3 went from 546× to 1.95×.

Finding 3 · GPTQ

GPTQ solves INT4 and INT3

GPTQ uses the Hessian matrix H = XXᵀ, computed from calibration text, to know which errors matter most, and compensates column by column. INT4 reaches 1.01× and INT3 1.15×: a 7B model at 3 bits would weigh ~2.6–3 GB.

Finding 4 · The INT2 wall

Damping the error breaks the wall

At 2 bits, GPTQ diverges (22,820×): compensation amplifies the error instead of cancelling it. Clipping that feedback at ±2.5σ (standard deviations) stabilizes it: from 288×, the best previous attempt, to 12.45× at clean 2 bits. The idea came from a swarm of 40 AI agents testing methods in parallel.

Finding 5 · Low-rank patch

Fixing the error with a few directions

The error GPTQ leaves behind is concentrated in a few output directions. A low-rank patch (a small correction made of r directions) recovers it: rank 8 gives 2.32× at 2.33 bits; rank 16, 1.54× at 2.67 bits; rank 32, 1.14× at 3.33 bits.

Negative results, documented

What failed is written down too

Protecting the largest 1–10% of weights barely moved INT2 (2,362× → 1,635×). Streaming weights from the SSD to run a 70B model took 35 seconds per token. And the family of low-rank corrections learned on calibration text, tested on new text with a larger model (Qwen 0.5B), did not generalize. That's why the 7B in ~2.4 GB is still a projection, not a result.

Beyond quantization

Other levers that were measured

Besides compressing weights, the research measured other ways to speed up inference (the moment an already-trained model generates answers).

Tree speculative decoding

A small model drafts several tokens and the large one checks them in a single pass: 1.8–2.75× faster, with the same quality.

2-bit kernel

A kernel is the program that runs inside the GPU. The 2-bit one ran 4.0× faster than FP16, at 53% of the hardware's theoretical limit.

E8 lattice vs. ternary

Quantizing weights in groups of 8 with the E8 lattice gave 4.51× less reconstruction error than ternary at the same bit budget, on a real 7B model. It's a quantizer-quality proxy, not perplexity.

The right model for each task

On simple tasks, small models nearly tie the 27B one and answer 70–100× faster. Routing by task is the cheapest lever.

Dropped: entropy compression

Compressing weights with entropy coding (rANS) saves space, but decoding was 8.74× slower.

Dropped: a home-grown compression law

An original idea (the "cone operator") promised huge factors, but on new text it fell to ~1×. It was documented and abandoned.

Under the hood

How it's built

Modest hardware, on purpose

A laptop with a 4 GB RTX 3050 (Ampere architecture), an Intel i5-12450H and ~32 GB of RAM. Everything is measured on the machine where the wall is real.

PyTorch, CUDA and Transformers

PyTorch 2.6 with CUDA 12.4 and the Hugging Face Transformers library to load models and measure perplexity.

One script per technique

The base lab, Lloyd-Max, GPTQ, rotations and the GPTQ + patch stack each live in separate, reproducible scripts.

Ollama testbed

PowerShell and Python scripts against the Ollama HTTP API; they log tokens per second, load time and processor to CSV.

A swarm of AI agents

40 AI agents explored ideas in parallel, each with its own method and result; a later blind review re-checked the speed-up figures.

A notebook with caveats

Numbered findings, negative results and warnings: for instance, 2-bit GPTQ is numerically sensitive (24× on GPU vs. 12.45× on CPU).

Engineering applied to efficiency

From the math (Hessians, Cholesky factorization, orthogonal rotations, lattices) to measurements on real hardware, with the negative results in plain sight.
