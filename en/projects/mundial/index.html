Sports machine learning

Predicting a World Cup with probabilities, not hunches.

A machine-learning model (software that learns patterns from data) estimates every 2026 World Cup match from more than 49,000 historical international matches and simulates the whole tournament 10,000 times. The web app shows the knockout bracket, the groups, the favorites and an honest validation against the real results.

In plain words

What it is and why it matters

What does it predict?

For each match: the probability of a win, draw or loss, the expected goals, and the minutes when goals are most likely. For the tournament: each team's probability of reaching every stage and of winning it all.

The problem

Football has few goals and a lot of luck. Saying "this team wins" hides the uncertainty, and a prediction is only worth something if it can be compared against a simple baseline and against what actually happened.

The solution

Combine several models, simulate thousands of tournaments to turn each match into advancement probabilities, and measure accuracy match by match using only information available before each game.

Step by step

From data to the knockout bracket

One command trains the models, simulates the tournament and produces the data the web app reads.

Data

International results, goalscorers and penalty shootouts from a public dataset, with historical team names normalized.

Team strength

A dynamic Elo rating plus an attack and defense measure for each team, with recent matches weighted more.

Models

XGBoost, a statistical scoreline model and Elo estimate each match; an ensemble combines them.

Simulation

Monte Carlo plays the tournament 10,000 times on the official bracket: scores, goal minutes, extra time and penalties.

The models

Four ways to look at a match

Each model can be selected in the app to build its own bracket and compare.

Elo

A strength score that goes up when you win and down when you lose, more so against strong opponents. Here it also weighs goal margin, home advantage and tournament importance.

Statistical (Dixon-Coles)

Models each team's goals with a Poisson distribution (the one that describes how often a rare event, like a goal, happens) and corrects low scores such as 0-0 and 1-0.

XGBoost

Many small decision trees, each one correcting the mistakes of the previous ones (gradient boosting). A classifier estimates win, draw or loss, and two regressors estimate each side's goals. Its features are Elo, attack and defense, recent form, neutral venue, tournament weight and geography (altitude and travel).

Ensemble

Combines Elo, the statistical model and XGBoost with weights tuned on a development set.

Monte Carlo simulation

Monte Carlo means repeating a random draw thousands of times to see how often each outcome happens. Scores are drawn from the expected goals; in the knockouts, draws go to extra time and penalties based on Elo, and the 8 best third-placed teams are slotted in following the official bracket rules.

Goal minutes

Minutes are drawn from the real distribution of thousands of historical goals: late goals are more likely.

Validation

How well it predicts

A football match has three possible outcomes, so random guessing is right about 33% of the time. Every figure comes from the public dataset bundled with the demo.

58.0%

653 matches from 15 World Cups

Walk-forward backtest (each match is predicted using only information from before it) from 1970 to 2026. The ensemble is right 58.0% of the time vs. 57.3% for Elo alone, and lowers log-loss from 0.957 to 0.949. Log-loss punishes being very confident and wrong: lower is better.

60.8%

97 matches from the 2026 World Cup

Predictions made before each match and compared with the real result: the model called the winner in 59 of 97 matches.

0.78 pts

Well-calibrated goal minutes

Across 15-minute windows, the predicted share of goals differs from the real one by 0.78 percentage points on average, measured over 2,826 goals.

Out

What didn't help didn't make it in

Penalties and head-to-head history were tested as features and dropped because they didn't improve accuracy. Data that only exists for 2026 (injuries, host advantage) is applied as an adjustment to future matches, never to training.

The app

Explore the tournament

A World Cup–style web app that reads the data generated by the model.

Interactive bracket

The road to the title in its most likely scenario. Opening a match shows the probability of advancing, the predicted score, a goal timeline and a heat map of when each team is most likely to score.

Pick the model

Build the bracket with the ensemble, XGBoost, the statistical model, Elo or a version trained on 2026 only.

Groups

Standings with real results: the top two of each of the 12 groups and the 8 best third-placed teams advance.

Favorites

Each team's probability of reaching every stage and of becoming champion.

Statistics

Tournament leaders in goals, assists, shots, distance covered and clean sheets.

Validation in plain sight

Every pre-match prediction next to the real result, with hits and misses marked. These are not live predictions: the demo keeps its cutoff date.

Under the hood

How it's built

Scientific Python

pandas, NumPy, SciPy, scikit-learn and XGBoost; tested from Python 3.11 up.

A reproducible pipeline

One script trains, simulates and writes JSON; the web app is static and needs no server of its own.

Separate collectors

Data APIs are only called by collection scripts that cache to disk; training reads local files and uses no quota.

No data leakage

Data leakage means training with information you wouldn't have before the match. That's why validation is walk-forward and the 2026 adjustments only touch future matches.

Ablations and experiments

Dedicated scripts test features, signals and weights one at a time; anything that doesn't improve validation stays as information, not as a predictor.

Framework-free frontend

Plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript with a dark theme; the official 48-team bracket, from the round of 32 to the final.
